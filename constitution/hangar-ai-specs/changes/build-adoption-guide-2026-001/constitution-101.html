Why this page exists

The Hangar AI Constitution is an AI governance model for agentic software development — not a style guide, not a plugin setting, not a folder of prompts. It defines laws, avatars, and skills that shape how a coding agent reasons, produces output, and participates in a governed SDLC.

Before most people engage with the guide they arrive with mental models built from traditional tooling: linters, prompt libraries, IDE extensions. Those models don't map cleanly onto constitutional AI. The questions below are the ones that come up repeatedly — from architects, from coaches, from engineers on day one.

Read the sections that match what's already in your head. You don't need to go in order.

Misconception 01

"This is just a coding style guide with extra steps."

❌ Misconception

"The Constitution is basically a big style guide. We already have coding standards. This is redundant overhead."

✅ Reality

A Constitutional AI system embeds organizational law directly into the AI model's context — so the model's outputs are governed, not just suggested.

The Terrain vs. the Map

A style guide tells humans what to do. The Constitution tells the AI what it IS and what it MUST produce. These are fundamentally different things.

  • Style guide: A human reads it, decides how much to follow, and writes code accordingly. Compliance is voluntary and inconsistent.
  • Constitution: The AI's context is the Constitution. Every output it produces is shaped by constitutional law — before the first line of code is written.

The Hangar AI Constitution defines four constitutional primitives:

  • Laws — what the AI must produce (168 in v2.0.0)
  • Avatars — who the AI is in each context
  • Phase gates — checkpoints that certify output
  • Amendment process — governed change control for the constitution itself
Analogy: A style guide is a map. The Constitution is the terrain itself. The AI doesn't read the map and decide whether to follow it — it lives in the terrain.

Every coding session under the Constitution is a constitutional session. The AI isn't just helping you code — it's producing governed, certifiable output under organizational law.

Deep Dive — Why context injection is more powerful than documentation

Large language models like GitHub Copilot process a context window — the full set of instructions and code visible during inference. When your constitutional document is injected into that context, the model doesn't "reference" it like a human reads a PDF. It processes every law, every guardrail, and every identity declaration as part of its active reasoning state.

This is why constitutional governance is categorically different from documentation governance. Documentation requires human attention and compliance discipline. Constitutional context injection is automatic, persistent, and active on every token the model generates.

The Constitution isn't a policy artifact. It's a runtime artifact.

Misconception 02

"Why is SOLID in the Constitution? Everyone already knows SOLID."

❌ Misconception

"SOLID is public knowledge. So are design patterns. Why is this in the Constitution? We're wasting tokens on things every developer knows."

✅ Reality

The Constitution isn't for humans to learn — it's for the AI model to be governed by. Public knowledge becomes organizational law when codified in the Constitution.

The AI Doesn't Know "Your" SOLID

Coding agents don't inherently apply SOLID in your context. They apply statistical patterns from training data drawn from millions of codebases — many of which violate SOLID outright, or apply it in ways incompatible with American Airlines' architectural standards.

  • Without constitutional governance, the AI applies SOLID on average — which means inconsistently, loosely, and without organizational specificity.
  • The Constitution makes SOLID (and AA's specific application of it) explicit, binding, and enforceable as law — not a suggestion the model can statistically ignore.
  • Without it, the AI will produce code that "works" but doesn't conform to your architectural standards.
The right question: "Do humans know SOLID?" is the wrong question. The right question is: "Does the AI apply SOLID in the way American Airlines requires?" The Constitution answers that — with force of law.

Public knowledge + organizational codification = organizational law. That's the entire point. The Constitution doesn't teach anyone SOLID. It commands the AI to apply it as AA defines it.

Deep Dive — How training data dilutes SOLID in practice

GitHub Copilot was trained on public repositories. A significant portion of those repositories contain procedural code, God Classes, tight coupling, and no meaningful separation of concerns. The model learned from all of it.

When you ask Copilot to "write a service class," its baseline is a statistical blend of every service class in its training data — good, bad, and mediocre. Constitutional laws like ARCH-1.1 (bounded-context scoping) and ENG-2.1 (single-responsibility enforcement) override that statistical average with organizational mandates.

This is why seemingly "obvious" principles must be codified. The model's default is the mean of its training set. The Constitution shifts that default to your organizational standard.

Misconception 03

"What does RLHF have to do with any of this?"

❌ Misconception

"RLHF is a model training concept. We're not training models. This is an adoption guide for developers, not ML engineers."

✅ Reality

The Constitution acts like a persistent, organizational RLHF signal — without retraining the model. It shapes preferences at inference time, every session.

Inference-Time Preference vs. Training-Time Preference

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is how models like GitHub Copilot are trained to prefer certain outputs over others. Human raters evaluate model outputs and signal which are better — the model learns to optimize for those preferences over millions of training examples.

Your organization cannot retrain Copilot. But you can provide a constitutional context that shapes every output — functioning like a persistent preference signal operating at inference time:

RLHF (Training Time)
Bakes preferences into model weights during training. Applies globally to all users. You cannot control it.
Constitution (Inference Time)
Bakes your preferences into every session via context injection. Applies specifically to your org. You control it completely.
  • RLHF trained the model to be helpful. The Constitution trains it to be helpful in your specific organizational context.
  • This is why the Constitution must be comprehensive. Gaps = ungoverned output. The model fills gaps with its training-set defaults.
  • The Constitution doesn't replace RLHF — it works alongside it, layering organizational specificity on top of the model's general helpfulness.
Implication: Every section of the Constitution that "feels obvious" is plugging a gap in the model's organizational alignment. Leave it out and the model reverts to its statistical default.
Deep Dive — Constitutional AI in the research literature

The term "Constitutional AI" originates in Anthropic's research (Bai et al., 2022), describing a technique where AI models are trained to follow a set of written principles. The Hangar AI Constitution adapts this concept to an operational context: rather than using the principles during model training, the Constitution is injected at inference time as organizational governance.

This is sometimes called "soft constitutional alignment" — the model isn't retrained with constitutional principles baked into weights, but instead receives those principles as authoritative context every session. The effect at the output level is similar: the model's responses are shaped by the constitutional framework as if it were a governing constraint.

The key difference from prompt engineering: a prompt can be ignored, abbreviated, or overridden by user input. A constitutional document, when properly structured and injected, establishes an authoritative context that the model treats as a governing framework — not a suggestion.

Misconception 04

"What's an Avatar? Is that just a workflow?"

❌ Misconception

"Avatar sounds like a workflow with a fancy name. Is this just another name for a prompt template?"

✅ Reality

An Avatar is the AI coding agent's constitutional identity — a nudge mechanism that establishes who the agent IS and what laws govern it. It is NOT a workflow.

Identity vs. Process — A Critical Distinction

An Avatar document defines four things about the AI agent before it writes a single line of code:

  • Persona — what role the agent plays (e.g., "Senior Architect at American Airlines")
  • Governing laws — which constitutional laws apply in this context, and which carry ⛔ non-negotiable status
  • Output bounds — what the agent can and cannot produce
  • Guardrails — the lines the agent must never cross, regardless of user instruction
Avatar (Identity)
What the agent IS. Active before and throughout the session. Defines constitutional identity. Cannot be overridden by workflow steps.
Workflow (Process)
What happens over time. A sequence of governed phases with gates. Uses an Avatar — is not an Avatar.

Think of it like a job description + code of conduct for the AI agent. Without an Avatar, the agent is ungoverned — it's helpful but constitutionally unbound. That's the risk.

Example: The "Senior Architect Avatar" tells the agent it IS a senior architect at American Airlines, governed by 168 laws, with DC-07 visibility required and bounded-context scoping mandatory. Every response it gives is shaped by that constitutional identity — regardless of what workflow phase is active.
Deep Dive — How Avatar nudge mechanisms work in practice

The term "nudge mechanism" comes from behavioral economics — a nudge shapes behavior by altering the decision environment without restricting options. An Avatar functions similarly: it shapes the model's "decision environment" (its context) so that constitutionally correct outputs become the path of least resistance.

When the Senior Architect Avatar is active, the model's context establishes: identity (who I am), laws (what I must do), scope (where I operate), and guardrails (what I must never do). The model doesn't "choose" to follow these — they're part of its active reasoning context. Outputs that violate the Avatar's constitutional identity require the model to work against its own context, which creates a strong inference-time pressure toward compliant outputs.

This is categorically different from a prompt that says "act as a senior architect." A prompt is a request. An Avatar is a constitutional declaration embedded in the model's governing context.

Misconception 05

"What's the difference between a law and a rule? Can't we just configure the rules?"

❌ Misconception

"Laws, rules — these are just names for configuration. We should be able to adjust them for our team's context."

✅ Reality

Laws are binding constitutional mandates — they cannot be skipped. Rules are contextual guidelines that adapt within the constitutional framework. ⛔ marks non-negotiables that apply even under stakeholder pressure.

The Constitutional Hierarchy

Type Count (v2.0.0) Configurable? What happens if violated?
Laws 168 No — always active Output is ungoverned. Phase gate fails.
Laws ⛔ Non-Negotiable 13 Absolutely not — no exceptions possible Constitutional breach. No stakeholder can authorize an exception.
Rules Variable Yes — within constitutional bounds Approach is sub-optimal; output remains governed.

Laws are organized by category — ENG-* (engineering), ARCH-* (architecture), SEC-* (security), and others. Some carry the ⛔ non-negotiable marker:

  • ENG-4.1 ⛔ — Test-Driven Development. TDD is not optional. The AI does not produce implementation before tests.
  • ENG-13.1 ⛔ — No external dependencies introduced without review. Zero tolerance.
When someone asks: "Can't we configure this?"
If it's a law: No. If it's a ⛔ law: Not under any circumstance, for any reason, from any authority. If it's a rule: Yes — but only within the bounds the governing laws set.

The principle: Laws govern the AI's output. Rules tune the AI's approach. You can tune the approach; you cannot negotiate the output.

Deep Dive — Why 13 non-negotiables and not more (or fewer)?

The 13 ⛔ non-negotiable laws were identified through constitutional deliberation as the minimum set of absolutes where any exception introduces systemic risk. These are not the "most important" laws — all 168 matter. They are the laws where the cost of a single exception (compounding debt, security breach, compliance failure, ungoverned drift) outweighs any benefit a business stakeholder might claim to justify the exception.

The ⛔ count is deliberately small. If everything is non-negotiable, nothing is. The 13 represent the constitutional floor — the bedrock below which the entire governance system loses integrity. Making additional laws non-negotiable requires the full amendment process, with evidence of systemic risk sufficient to justify the absolute restriction.

Misconception 06

"How do we change the Constitution? Can't we just edit the file?"

❌ Misconception

"It's a text file. If we need to update a law, we update the file. This amendment process sounds like unnecessary bureaucracy."

✅ Reality

The Constitution governs its own amendment. Changes require PROPOSE → jury deliberation → IMPLEMENT → ARCHIVE. Editing the file without this process produces an ungoverned fork — not a constitutional update.

The Amendment Process — Why Friction Is a Feature

An AI-governed system that can be changed ad-hoc is not actually governed — it's just a config file. The amendment process exists to ensure that changes to the governing framework are themselves governed.

1

PROPOSE

Author a formal proposal: hypothesis, design constraints, evidence of need. Submit to the governing body. Minor amendments have lighter evidence requirements; major amendments require full Stage B–F evidence.

2

Jury Deliberation

A 6-juror panel reviews the proposal over 2+ rounds:

  • Jurors: Architect · Coach · Engineer · Product Owner · Compliance · Legal
  • Round 1 must surface concerns — unanimous first-round approval is constitutionally invalid
  • Concerns must be addressed before the proposal advances
3

IMPLEMENT

Build the change under constitutional governance itself — governed by the very constitution you're amending. The change is not ratified until implementation is complete and evidence artifacts are filed.

4

ARCHIVE

Evidence artifacts, stage outcomes, and the jury record are filed and made immutable. The amended constitution is versioned. The archive is the constitutional record.

"But we just need a small change." Small ungoverned changes compound into drift. The process scales: minor amendments have lighter evidence requirements. The friction is proportional — but it is never zero. That's intentional.
Deep Dive — Why unanimous first-round approval is constitutionally invalid

This is one of the Constitution's most counterintuitive rules — and one of its most important. If a 6-person jury unanimously approves a constitutional amendment in the first round, it means no one surfaced a concern. In a system governing AI output across an organization, that's almost certainly false consensus — not genuine agreement.

Constitutional amendments carry systemic risk. If the amendment is sound, it can survive a round of structured concerns. If it cannot, those concerns caught a real problem before it became a constitutional flaw. Requiring Round 1 concerns forces deliberation — even when a proposal seems obviously correct. "Obviously correct" proposals have historically introduced the most consequential unforeseen problems.

This rule doesn't slow down good proposals. It surfaces hidden risks in proposals that look good on first read.

Misconception 07

"The AI just writes code. What's 'governed output'?"

❌ Misconception

"The AI produces code. We review it. We ship it. What else is there? Governed output sounds like documentation theater."

✅ Reality

Governed output = code + phase gate evidence + test artifacts + compliance documentation — all produced under constitutional law and certifiable. It's not theater; it's a constitutional paper trail.

Ungoverned vs. Governed — Side by Side

Ungoverned Output

  • AI produces code
  • Developer reviews it
  • PR is opened
  • Code is shipped
  • Hope it's good
Governed Output

  • AI produces code under constitutional law
  • Phase gate evidence artifact filed
  • Test artifacts: characterization tests + mutation results
  • Compliance documentation: SonarQube gate + bounded-context declaration
  • PR has a constitutional paper trail

The output is not just functional — it is certifiable. You can point to the phase gate evidence and say: "This output was produced under constitutional governance. Here is the proof."

This matters for:

  • Regulatory compliance — demonstrable evidence of governed process
  • Audit trails — immutable record of what was decided and why
  • Team onboarding — new engineers can read the evidence artifacts to understand architectural decisions
  • AI model drift detection — governed output enables comparison across sessions to detect when the model's behavior drifts from constitutional expectations
The goal: Every AI-assisted PR has a constitutional paper trail. Not as overhead — as confidence. The paper trail is the difference between "I think this is right" and "I can prove this is governed."
Deep Dive — What a phase gate evidence artifact contains

A phase gate evidence artifact is a structured document produced at the completion of each workflow phase. It contains:

  • Phase summary — what was attempted, what was produced, what was deferred
  • Constitutional decisions — which laws were active, which were most relevant, and how they shaped the output
  • Architectural decisions — bounded-context declaration, dependency decisions, SOLID compliance statement
  • Test evidence — characterization test results, mutation test score, coverage delta
  • Drift signals — early indicators of future drift to monitor in subsequent phases
  • Gate status — PASS / CONDITIONAL / FAIL with explicit justification

The artifact is not a status update — it's a constitutional record. It exists to make AI-assisted development auditable, reproducible in terms of process, and trusted across team members who weren't present during the session.

Misconception 08

"I can follow the workflow like a recipe and always get the same result, right?"

❌ Misconception

"Workflows are step-by-step. If I follow the steps, I'll get deterministic, reproducible output every time."

✅ Reality

Workflows are governed non-deterministic flows. The AI may take different paths — but every path must clear the phase gate. The Constitution governs the outcome, not the route.

The Airport Security Analogy

Coding agents are not deterministic. The same prompt can produce different code on different runs. This is fundamental to how large language models work — they sample from probability distributions, not lookup tables.

The analogy: Different travelers take different routes to the airport — some drive, some take the train, some arrive early, some late. But everyone must clear the same security checkpoint. The checkpoint doesn't care about the route. It checks what you're bringing through.

Workflows don't eliminate non-determinism — they govern it. Each phase has a gate. The gate checks the output (evidence artifacts, test results, architectural decisions) — not the path the AI took to produce it.

  • Non-deterministic: The specific code the AI writes in Phase 2 may vary between sessions
  • Deterministic: The phase gate requirements are fixed. The evidence artifact format is fixed. The constitutional laws that govern the output are fixed.
  • The gate is the constitutional check — it's not bureaucracy, it's the mechanism that makes non-deterministic AI output trustworthy
Implication: Don't expect identical step-by-step reproducibility. Expect governed, certifiable outcomes at each gate. The gate is where constitutional governance manifests — not the route the model took.
Deep Dive — Temperature, sampling, and why identical prompts produce different code

Language models generate text by predicting the probability distribution of the next token, then sampling from that distribution. The "temperature" parameter controls how much randomness is introduced during sampling. At temperature 0, the model always picks the most probable token (greedy decoding). At higher temperatures, lower-probability tokens are selected more frequently, introducing variation.

GitHub Copilot and most coding assistants operate at non-zero temperatures for practical reasons: greedy decoding produces repetitive, less creative output that often gets stuck in local optima. The randomness that makes the AI feel "smart" is also what makes it non-deterministic.

Constitutional governance accounts for this by governing outputs rather than paths. Phase gate evidence is output-focused: what tests pass, what architectural decisions were made, what laws are satisfied. These can be verified regardless of which exact code path the model took.

Misconception 09

"I want to create mini-workflows as skills to speed things up."

❌ Misconception

"The workflows feel long. I can collapse them into faster skills — same output, less overhead. Mini-workflows are just efficient workflows."

✅ Reality

Skills are atomic capabilities. Workflows are multi-phase governed processes. A skill feeds INTO a phase — it cannot replace a phase gate. "Mini-workflows" that skip gates produce fast, ungoverned output.

Skills Are Tools. Workflows Are the Governed Process.

A Skill

A single atomic action. One prompt → one output → one capability.

Example: "Scan this class for law violations" — one action, one result.
A Workflow

A sequence of governed phases, each with a gate, evidence requirement, and drift signals. Phases build on each other.

Example: "Legacy Rescue" — 7 phases, each certifying progress.

A "mini-workflow" that collapses 7 phases into 1 skips all the intermediate gates. The consequences:

  • ❌ No phase gate evidence at intermediate stages
  • ❌ No drift detection between phases
  • ❌ No constitutional certification of accumulated decisions
  • ❌ Output looks fast — but is ungoverned

The right mental model: Skills are tools. Workflows are the governed process that uses those tools. Skills accelerate work within a phase. They don't replace the phase structure.

Stop signal: If you're building a "skill" that spans multiple phases — stop. What you actually need is a new workflow — which requires the formal amendment process described in Section 6. A new workflow is a constitutional artifact, not a quick-win shortcut.
Deep Dive — Why phase gates can't be compressed

Phase gates don't just check the output of a single action — they certify the accumulated constitutional integrity of everything produced up to that point. A gate at Phase 3 validates not just Phase 3 outputs, but that Phase 2's evidence was properly filed, that drift signals from Phase 1 were addressed, and that the architectural decisions at each phase are coherent across the whole.

Compressing phases loses this accumulated certification. You get code that passes phase-3-level tests without phase-1 and phase-2 governance evidence in the record. The result is output that looks governed (it passes the final check) but isn't governed (the intermediate constitutional decisions were never certified).

This is especially dangerous in legacy rescue and refactoring scenarios, where each phase builds on the bounded-context declaration from the previous phase. Skipping intermediate gates means the bounded context was never formally certified — creating an ungoverned foundation for all subsequent phases.

Misconception 10

"Just use GitHub Copilot skills — that's the same thing as the Constitution."

❌ Misconception

"We already have GitHub Copilot. I can define agent skills and custom instructions there. Why do we need the Hangar AI Constitution on top of that? They're the same thing."

✅ Reality

GitHub Copilot skills are prompt customizations scoped to a developer's session. Constitutional skills are governed behavioral contracts — ratified by a jury, tied to 168 laws, consistent across every team and every agent session. One is a setting. The other is an institution.

Two Very Different Things With Similar Names

GitHub Copilot Skills / Custom Instructions

  • Defined by an individual developer or team
  • Scoped to a project, repo, or session
  • No governance process — anyone can write them
  • No laws — the agent follows the instruction but has no constitutional obligation
  • No phase gates — output has no certification trail
  • No jury — no deliberation, no PROPOSE→IMPLEMENT process
  • No RLHF feedback loop — model behavior doesn't shift with your team's usage patterns
  • Siloed — your custom prompt doesn't benefit another team
Hangar AI Constitutional Skills

  • Ratified through formal PROPOSE → 6-juror jury → IMPLEMENT → ARCHIVE governance
  • Bound by 168 constitutional laws — the agent cannot violate them
  • Shared across every team adopting the constitution — consistent behavior organization-wide
  • Phase-gated — skills produce certified, evidenced outputs
  • Connected to avatars — skill behavior is shaped by the product and technology context
  • Part of the RLHF feedback loop — governed output trains better governed output
  • Auditable — every skill artifact has a constitutional record

The Real Distinction: Customization vs Governance

GitHub Copilot skills let you customize what the agent focuses on in your session. That's useful. The Hangar AI Constitution governs how the agent reasons, what it can't do, and what evidence it must produce — regardless of who is using it or what session they're in.

Copilot skill example

"Always use our company's API naming convention."

Effect: Agent follows the instruction during this session. No enforcement across teams. No audit trail. Tomorrow's session may or may not have this instruction active.
Constitutional skill example

Skill: atomic-tdd — governed by ENG-4.1 ⛔ (non-negotiable TDD law).

Effect: Agent cannot generate production code without a failing test first — in every session, for every team, enforced by law. Violation is a blocked phase gate. Evidence is filed.
Why this matters for architects: Recommending "just use Copilot skills" in place of the Constitution removes the governance layer entirely. You get a faster agent — but an ungoverned one. The output has no constitutional certification, no law enforcement, no phase gate record. At scale, across 50 teams, this produces 50 different agent behaviors with 0 organizational consistency and 0 audit trail.

Can You Use Both?

Yes — and you should. GitHub Copilot's custom instructions and skills are a delivery mechanism. The Hangar AI Constitution provides the governance model that runs on top of that mechanism. Constitutional skills are authored as governed artifacts and then loaded into the agent session alongside whatever Copilot configuration your team uses. They complement each other — the Constitution isn't replacing Copilot, it's governing how Copilot operates within your SDLC.

Deep Dive — Why "prompt customization" and "constitutional governance" are categorically different

Prompt customization (Copilot skills, custom instructions, system prompts) operates at the instruction layer. The agent receives a preferred behavior and tries to follow it. There is no enforcement mechanism — if the model's next-token prediction pushes against the instruction, it may deviate. There is no audit of whether the instruction was followed. There is no consequence for violation.

Constitutional governance operates at the behavioral contract layer. Laws define what the agent must and cannot do. Phase gates create checkpoints where a human or automated reviewer verifies that the contract was honored. The jury process ensures that the constitutional artifacts themselves were deliberated before being included. The RLHF loop means that governed sessions produce training signal that reinforces the constitutional behavior in future model versions.

The difference is the difference between a "please try to follow this convention" note in a README and a CI gate that fails the build when the convention is violated. Both exist in your project — but only one enforces.
