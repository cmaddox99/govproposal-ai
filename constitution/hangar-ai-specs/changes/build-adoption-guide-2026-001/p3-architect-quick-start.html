🤖 What is the agent doing? DC-03

🤖 Agent Role Explainer DC-03

The constitutional agent follows a strict three-phase cycle: PROPOSE → IMPLEMENT → ARCHIVE. Here's exactly what that means for architecture work.
📖
PROPOSE — Agent reads and analyses
The agent reads the constitution and analyses your architecture against the engineering laws. It produces a structured proposal — bounded contexts identified, law violations flagged, workflow recommendation made — with full law citations. Nothing has changed in the codebase yet.
Law citations are auditable commitments. If a citation is wrong, object before approving.
🧭
[Human gate] — You review the proposal
🏗️
Your review checklist: Are the law IDs correct? Is the scope (bounded context) appropriate? Is the recommended workflow the right one for this situation? Do the identified violations match what you'd expect?
You can reduce scope, request a different bounded context, or ask the agent to re-analyse before approving.
🔨
IMPLEMENT — Agent acts on approval
For an architecture scan, the implement phase is usually read-only — it produces a report and architecture decision record, not code changes. For adoption or legacy rescue workflows, this phase makes real repo changes. Either way, it only proceeds after your explicit approval.
The agent never makes irreversible changes without an approved proposal on record (BUS-1.1 ⛔).
📁
ARCHIVE — Evidence committed
The evidence artifact is committed to hangar-ai-specs/. You now have an auditable architecture decision record: which laws were checked, what was found, who approved, and what was done. This is the constitutional audit trail (ENG-11.2).
Every future workflow can reference these phase artifacts as baseline evidence.
🧭 Human Agency & Adaptive Scope DC-07

🧭 Human Agency & Adaptive Scope DC-07

As architect, you set the scope. The constitution governs by design — not by mandate. Here's what that means for your decisions.
1
You govern the scope
The constitution suggests the full picture. You define the bounded context. Running legacy rescue on one package is a valid, complete constitutional outcome. You are not required to fix everything at once — the constitution is designed to run iteratively by bounded context.
2
Law precedence is your call to understand
When two laws appear to conflict in a proposal, the constitution's precedence hierarchy governs. Non-negotiable (⛔) laws always win. If you believe a law is wrong for your context, you can propose a local law override or formal amendment — see Step 5 below. Never work around a ⛔ law without a formal amendment in place.
3
Proposals are not code reviews
A constitutional proposal is a governance decision, not a line-by-line code review. When reviewing a proposal, focus on: Does this scope make sense for our bounded context? Are the law citations correct? Is the recommended workflow appropriate? Leave detailed code review to the subsequent PR process.
4
SonarQube gate = no new debt
The quality gate enforces no new violations in this changeset. Existing violations become the baseline — you don't have to fix the whole codebase before adopting the constitution. The gate protects against regression; it does not demand perfection on day one.
DC-11 Non-determinism: Constitutional workflows are probabilistic guidance systems, not deterministic scripts. The same prompt run twice may produce different proposals — that is by design. Always review before approving, even for familiar workflows.
5
Bounded context scoping
Scope any workflow to one module, package, or service boundary. The constitution runs iteratively by design. A single bounded context adoption is a complete, first-class constitutional outcome. Use the architecture scan (Step 2 above) to identify your best starting bounded context.
📐 Architect Adoption Path DC-01

The Hangar AI Constitution is organised into four main areas:

📂 laws/ — 168 laws across 3 domains: ENG (engineering), PRD (product), BUS (business/governance)
📂 workflows/ — 5 workflows: adoption, legacy rescue, greenfield, phase gate, amendment
📄 AGENTS.md — AI agent instructions. This is what the agent reads to govern its behaviour in your repo
📂 hangar-ai-specs/ — your evidence directory. Every phase gate artifact lives here after commit

Run this prompt to get an architect-level orientation:

Summarise the Hangar AI Constitution structure. What are the 3 law domains? What are the non-negotiable laws that apply to architecture decisions?

The adoption workflow is the entry point for any codebase. It creates AGENTS.md (the AI governance file) and initialises the hangar-ai-specs/ directory structure. Without adoption, no other constitutional workflow has a governed baseline to operate from.

Scope adoption to a single bounded context first. Once adoption is complete in one module, all other workflows (legacy rescue, greenfield, phase gate) can reference it as their anchor.

→ Open the Adoption Workflow

Every constitutional workflow phase produces a structured HTML evidence artifact committed to hangar-ai-specs/. These artifacts contain the decisions the AI made, the laws cited, the scope agreed, and the human approval record.

As architect, you need to be able to read these artifacts fluently — they are the governance record for your team's constitutional work. The Phase Gate Evidence Guide explains every field and what to look for.

→ Open the Phase Gate Evidence Guide

Technical coaches use the P3 Coach page to onboard teams into constitutional workflows. As architect, your governance role is different: you ensure that the proposals the agent generates cite the correct laws for your architectural context.

When reviewing a proposal from your team, check: Does the bounded context match the actual service boundary? Are the ENG law citations applicable to this module's architecture pattern? Is the recommended workflow (adoption vs legacy rescue) the right one for this team's situation?

You do not need to attend every session — but teams should know they can escalate a proposal to you if the law citations look wrong for the architecture.

→ Workflows Hub — all 5 workflows

If a law doesn't fit your architecture as written, you have two options: a local law override (scoped to one repo, documented in AGENTS.md) or a formal amendment (proposed change to the constitution itself via the P10 amendment process).

Use this prompt to get the agent's advice on whether an override or amendment is appropriate:

I believe law ENG-3.1 as written may cause suboptimal design in this codebase. Review this law against our architecture and advise whether a local law override or formal amendment is appropriate.

Note: ⛔ non-negotiable laws cannot be locally overridden — only a formal amendment can change them. The amendment process (P10) is coming next in the Adoption Guide.

▼ Coaching Notes for Architects DC-06
▼ Coaching Notes for Architects — Socratic prompts for experienced practitioners DC-06 · expand to read
  1. Bounded context selection: What bounded context should you scope this workflow to? What would happen to team confidence if you tried to run it on the entire repo first? How would you communicate the decision to limit scope — as a constraint, or as a deliberate architectural choice?
  2. Non-negotiable constraints: Which non-negotiable laws (⛔) create the hardest constraints for your current architecture? Which ones could you work with most easily? If a ⛔ law conflicts with an existing architectural decision, what is the right escalation path?
  3. Law conflict resolution: If two law IDs appear to conflict in a proposal, how would you resolve that before approving? What would you look for in the constitution's precedence hierarchy? When does a conflict become an amendment trigger?
  4. Phase artifact drift: What would it mean for a phase artifact to "show drift"? What would you look for — a gap between the proposal's bounded context and the code changes actually made? A law citation that doesn't match the domain of the change? An ARCHIVE record with no matching PROPOSE record?
  5. Explaining the cycle: How would you explain the PROPOSE → IMPLEMENT → ARCHIVE cycle to a new engineer who has never seen a constitutionally-governed PR? What analogy would you use to explain why the proposal must exist before implementation — not after?
